# Copy the plumbing, not the LLM traders

As of October 2026, no LLM-driven stock trader worth replicating has a verified edge over buy-and-hold. The 20-year bias-controlled test (FINSABER, KDD 2026) shows published LLM agents lose to buy-and-hold with high significance. Live real-money arenas show mostly losses: 4 of 6 models lost in Alpha Arena Season 1 and only 6 of 32 runs were profitable in Season 1.5. No fund or Claude-driven account has audited multi-month returns. What *is* worth replicating is the engineering the best projects converged on independently. In that pattern an LLM emits at most a bounded, auditable signal, while deterministic code owns sizing, hard risk clamps, cost-modeled fills, an order gate, a kill switch and an append-only ledger. The strategy layer should come from the narrow set of systematic, multi-stock themes with replicated out-of-sample evidence, not from the day-trading setups that look good in demos. Nick's own rounds 1–2 already fit the literature's base rate. Opening-range breakout and the day-trader-skill scanner setups lost after costs on about 100 large caps. Connors RSI(2) on SPY passed out of sample (1993–2016, 179 trades, +0.61%/trade, t=5.3), close to an independent +0.69%/trade test. One constraint shapes the architecture: Claude will not itself submit real-money orders through a broker connector, including the Robinhood MCP. Real-money autonomy therefore has to come from a deterministic program Nick deploys with his own broker credentials, or from Nick placing or approving orders. Claude can run paper and shadow trading end to end and handle research, monitoring and review. The recommended build is a monthly multi-sleeve engine (ETF trend, vol-scaled large-cap momentum, index RSI(2) mean reversion) executed by code, with Claude as analyst, paper trader and auditor.

## Twenty years of data say LLM agents trail buy-and-hold

The strongest long-horizon evidence is negative. FINSABER (Li, Kim, Cucuringu and Ma, accepted at KDD 2026) tested FinMem and FinAgent over **2004–2024 on more than 7,000 US equities including delisted names**, with rolling reselection of historical S&P 500 constituents ([arXiv v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v5)). Buy-and-hold beat both agents on Sharpe, with paired t-test **p-values between 3e-6 and 0.0117**. Neither agent showed significant CAPM alpha, and FinMem's alpha was negative in every scenario. The failure depends on the regime. In bull years buy-and-hold posted Sharpe 0.61 against FinAgent's 0.12 and FinMem's −0.19, and the agents also lost more in bear years. Cheap baselines such as ARIMA and buy-and-hold on a low-volatility universe beat them outright ([arXiv v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v5)). The authors' prescription is regime awareness and adaptive risk control "rather than adding framework complexity" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.07078)). When FINSABER re-ran FinMem's own headline TSLA window, it got Sharpe 0.927, not the 2.679 originally reported.

The reason older backtests look good is memorization, and it cannot be prompted away. GPT-4o recalls pre-cutoff S&P 500 levels at **0.61% error with 80.6% directional accuracy, versus 16.9% error and 45.7% after the cutoff** ([Lopez-Lira, Tang, Zhu](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v2)). Telling the model to ignore the future fails, and so does masking firm names: anonymized Apple, Meta and Microsoft earnings calls were re-identified 100% of the time. Profit Mirage moved five agent frameworks, TradingAgents among them, past GPT-4o's cutoff and measured **51–62% Sharpe decay and 50–72% total-return decay** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07920v1)). The Alpha Illusion re-ran TradingAgents for all of 2025 on five stocks with commissions, tokens, spread and impact included. Net Sharpe fell from 0.43 to **0.22**, and the agent ended at $102.3K against buy-and-hold's $104.8K; net buy-and-hold beat both agents tested on 4 of 5 tickers ([alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.16895.md)). The same paper found that 35 of 40 system-by-friction cells across reviewed frameworks were simply unmodeled.

Contamination-free benchmarks tell the same story over shorter windows. StockBench's best agents made about 2.5% over 82 trading days in 2025 against a 0.4% passive baseline, but with no costs and only 3 seeds. Every agent lagged in the downturn and led in the upturn, which is beta, not skill ([StockBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v2)). LiveTradeBench found that a model's LMArena score does not predict its trading results (Spearman 0.054 on stocks) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03628v1)). CLQT is the most rigorous design, with point-in-time data, four market-impact models and repeated runs. It measured a run-to-run **noise floor of about ±0.42 Sharpe** and found that agents "do not clearly beat the cap-weighted index". Scaffolded, structured runs did far better than autonomous ones: the structured runs beat about 6 of 8 passive baselines, while the autonomous runs beat only 1 ([CLQT](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.29771v1)). Agent Market Arena reached the same conclusion from live trading: the framework drives behavior far more than the backbone model ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11695)).

The defensible positive evidence is narrower. **LLMs work as text-to-signal components, not end-to-end timers.** GPT-4 headline scores predict post-cutoff drift, mainly in small stocks and on negative news, and the strategy's returns fall as LLM adoption spreads ([Lopez-Lira & Tang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07619v6)). Chronologically consistent models such as ChronoGPT show that the news channel survives without look-ahead ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.21206v2)). The best-known end-to-end claim, that GPT-4 beats analysts on financial statements, was withdrawn after replication found data inconsistencies ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17866v3)). The practical implication for Nick: any LLM-in-the-loop backtest overlapping the model's training window tells him nothing about skill. An LLM component's value can be established only by forward paper trading, and with a ±0.4 Sharpe noise floor that takes years, not weeks.

Three lessons transfer directly. First, **overtrading kills**. Second, **Claude leans long**: it ran a 100% long book in Season 1 and showed a bullish bias in Season 1.5 ([Buildix](https://www.buildix.trade/blog/nof1-alpha-arena-lessons-ai-trading-byok-strategy-advisor-2026); [BigGo/Bloomberg](https://finance.biggo.com/news/5BSX_Z0BaoGGrU-ID27J)). Flat Circle's survey of 11 arenas found Claude strong at forecasting but often weakest at investing ([Flat Circle](https://blog.flatcircle.ai/p/ai-trading-arenas)). Third, **concentration sinks accounts regardless of skill**: Smith's AYTR position fell 83% after a failed trial ([Flat Circle](https://blog.flatcircle.ai/p/ai-trading-arenas)). Nof1's founder names timing, sizing and overtrading as the core weaknesses ([BigGo/Bloomberg](https://finance.biggo.com/news/5BSX_Z0BaoGGrU-ID27J)). A deterministic engine eliminates all three by construction. In long-only stock arenas, "AI profitability" mostly tracks a rising market. One industry observer says none of the AI bots he tracks has shown persistent excess returns, and no audited LLM-agent fund exists ([BigGo/Bloomberg](https://finance.biggo.com/news/5BSX_Z0BaoGGrU-ID27J)).

## The best frameworks converged on LLM-signal, code-decides

The open-source field splits into star magnets and well-engineered code, and these are not the same projects. TradingAgents (114,789 stars, Apache-2.0) and ai-hedge-fund (64,260 stars, MIT) dominate attention, followed by the fast-rising Vibe-Trading (36,697 stars since April 2026, MIT) ([GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/TauricResearch/TradingAgents); [GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/virattt/ai-hedge-fund); [GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/HKUDS/Vibe-Trading)). Most of them copy a trading-firm org chart: parallel analysts, a bull/bear debate, a trader, risk review and a portfolio manager. That shape buys interpretability, not edge. TradingAgents' headline Sharpe of 8.21 on AAPL came from 3 tickers over about 3 months with no stated costs ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.20138)). An independent recomputation found the paper's buy-and-hold baseline sign was wrong, +9.12% rather than −5.23% ([DEV](https://dev.to/trow126/the-most-starred-llm-trading-paper-claims-buy-and-hold-lost-523-it-actually-gained-912-1jj6)). A GitHub issue documented live look-ahead during backtests that made "every day's decision… BUY" ([Issue #203](https://github.com/TauricResearch/TradingAgents/issues/203)). Its own README now calls it a "research scaffold" with no replicable return ([README](https://github.com/TauricResearch/TradingAgents)). Older academic repos (FinMem, FinAgent) have been unmaintained since 2024 ([GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/pipiku915/FinMem-LLM-StockTrading)).

The parts worth copying are the plumbing, and they sit in specific files. ai-hedge-fund's `risk/limits.py` states the core principle: "Conviction requests, risk disposes… the LLM's influence over the book ends at the Signal, and no clamp is ever negotiable." It enforces `max_position_pct` and `max_gross_exposure`, sends clamped exposure to cash, and logs every `ClampEvent` ([limits.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/virattt/ai-hedge-fund/HEAD/hedge_fund/risk/limits.py)). Its LLM contract fails loud on data errors and **abstains on LLM or parse failure**. It also caches every prompt and response, and offers a "blind" backtest mode that withholds ticker, dates and dollar values ([llm_agent.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/virattt/ai-hedge-fund/HEAD/hedge_fund/signals/llm_agent.py)). Its cost model defaults to 5 bps half-spread plus $0.005/share ([costs.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/virattt/ai-hedge-fund/HEAD/hedge_fund/brokers/costs.py)). Paper NAV lives in a hash-chained ledger "so NAV is a track record, not a reset" ([README](https://github.com/virattt/ai-hedge-fund)). TradingAgents contributes a settle-then-reflect decision log that scores each past call on realized alpha once its horizon passes, and it enters at the first close after the decision ([README](https://github.com/TauricResearch/TradingAgents)).

Vibe-Trading matters most to anyone wiring a real broker. Its Alpaca **order gate "durably owns every Alpaca submission before the broker write"** and recovers lost responses. Its kill switch survives restarts and "stops trusting a flaky broker", and runners carry persistent daily LLM-spend budgets ([README](https://github.com/HKUDS/Vibe-Trading)). Its changelog reads as a catalogue of execution bugs: a gate that refused every real order while its tests passed, a limit order sized off the quote that could fill at 2x, a malformed broker reply "that counted as an empty account", dividends booked as losses, and a momentum factor "that read today's price" ([README](https://github.com/HKUDS/Vibe-Trading)). The empty-account bug is the same failure Nick's CLAUDE.md forbids, an upstream failure reported as an empty result. Nick should read that changelog in full before writing a line of execution code. Cost is the remaining caveat. A TradingAgents analysis runs roughly **$0.20–$0.80 per ticker per run** on gpt-4o-class models with one debate round, so a 50-ticker daily debate would cost about $10–40 a day before any evidence that it adds value ([Railway](https://railway.com/deploy/trading-agents)).

## Claude can run paper trading end to end, but not real-money orders

Every public Claude trading setup is either human-gated or paper-only, and none has a verified track record. Jake Schincariol's day-trader-skill, the source of Nick's round-2 scanner setups, is a pipeline of 11 slash-command skills sharing state through a `trader/` folder. Its Python `check.py` returns PASS or BLOCK on max loss per trade, daily loss, trades per day, reward-to-risk and position size. Orders go to Alpaca paper only after a PASS *and* the user typing "yes" ([GitHub](https://github.com/Jakeschincariol/day-trader-skill)). The author's own replay of his headline TSLA opening-range breakout gave 13 targets against 15 stops in 33 signals, which he calls "close to a coin flip" ([GitHub](https://github.com/Jakeschincariol/day-trader-skill)). That is consistent with Nick's finding that the setups lose after costs. Unattended builds such as satyamdas03/alpaca-trading-agent use stateless scheduled sessions that read git-tracked memory files (`STRATEGY.md`, `TRADE_LOG.md`, `LESSONS_LEARNED.md`), on Alpaca paper, with no published returns ([GitHub](https://github.com/satyamdas03/alpaca-trading-agent)). The most honest long-run write-up is Chudi Nnorukam's Polyphemus Polymarket bot. There Claude Code writes the bot but never decides trades. About $1,300 was lost to ungated changes, including a `set("BTC")` that evaluates to `{'B','T','C'}`. Promotion now requires a shadow instance with at least 50 completed trades, and the author says the bot "has not made money" ([chudi.dev](https://chudi.dev/blog/claude-code-production-trading-bot)).

The constraint Nick needs to design around is that **Claude itself will not submit real-money orders through a broker connector**, the Robinhood MCP included. Builders have reported this since late August 2026: Claude told one Robinhood agentic-account user it "wasn't allowed to execute real trades" under "a standing safety rule", even with explicit authorization ([Saving to Invest](https://savingtoinvest.com/i-let-claude-trade-my-robinhood-agentic-account-heres-what-happened/)). The scheduling platform reinforces the same boundary. Claude Code cloud scheduled tasks run in auto mode, where a classifier can block actions, and "the fired prompt… can't act as approval or consent for actions during the run" ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/routines)). Real-money autonomy therefore has to come from one of two places. One is a deterministic program Nick writes, reviews and deploys with his own broker credentials. The other is Nick placing or approving the orders himself. Claude can legitimately do everything else end to end. It can run the paper or shadow book (Alpaca paper fills, or a local ledger priced off live Robinhood quotes), research and backtest new sleeves, reconcile the ledger against broker positions, read run transcripts, flag anomalies, and write the weekly review.

The scheduling mechanics matter for that Claude-side work. Cloud scheduled tasks reject cadences faster than hourly. Runs on the hour "can start several minutes late", five-field cron runs in UTC unless prefixed with `CRON_TZ=`, and a "Succeeded" status means only that the session ended, not that the work happened ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/routines)). That fits a few fixed checkpoints per day for a monthly or daily engine, not intraday loops. In-session crons expire after 7 days, so builders move durable schedules to the OS scheduler or a VPS ([GitHub](https://github.com/satyamdas03/alpaca-trading-agent)). The real-money engine itself should never depend on an LLM session starting on time. It should run under systemd or Task Scheduler with a heartbeat, as Polyphemus does ([chudi.dev](https://chudi.dev/blog/claude-code-production-trading-bot)).

## Robinhood allows agent trading; Alpaca suits a deterministic engine

Robinhood's Agentic Trading, in beta since 2026-05-27, is officially sanctioned automated trading. Users fund a dedicated account that external agents reach through Robinhood's Trading MCP server, and the agent can trade only that account's money ([Robinhood Newsroom](https://robinhood.com/newsroom/robinhood-is-now-open-to-agents/)). **Trade approvals default OFF for MCP accounts**, so an agent "can place eligible trades without per-order confirmation". But approvals are "still rolling out", some trades "may still require your approval", and multi-leg options are excluded ([Robinhood Support](https://robinhood.com/us/en/support/articles/trading-with-your-agent/)). The connector attached to Nick's sessions exposes `review_equity_order`, `place_equity_order`, `place_advanced_order` and `get_trade_approval_setting`, plus historicals and tax-lot tools. Three gaps make it a poor foundation for a deterministic engine. It has **no paper mode** ([Robinhood Newsroom](https://robinhood.com/newsroom/robinhood-is-now-open-to-agents/)), no documented request/response schemas ([NexusTrade](https://nexustrade.io/blog/robinhood-agentic-trading-mcp-review-20260708)), and no classic public equity REST API. The account is also reportedly cash-only ([Finder](https://www.finder.com/stock-trading/robinhood-agentic-accounts)), so T+1 settlement limits how fast a mean-reversion sleeve can recycle capital. A program of Nick's own could in principle act as an MCP client against `agent.robinhood.com/mcp/trading` under his own login ([Gamut](https://www.gamut.so/mcp/payments-finance/robinhood)). That would run on undocumented auth inside a beta that Robinhood itself warns "may be hard to stop in real time", so treat it as experimental.

Alpaca's paper and live accounts share one API, and its order classes match a close-executed multi-stock engine. It offers bracket, OCO and OTO orders, fractional shares (day TIF only), and `cls` market-on-close orders, which must be submitted **before 3:50pm ET** ([Alpaca docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/orders-at-alpaca)). For the survivorship-free backtests below, minute-aggregate flat files go back to 2003 on Massive (formerly Polygon) ([Massive docs](https://massive.com/docs/flat-files/stocks/minute-aggregates)), and Databento's usage-based billing includes $125 of free credits for a one-off pull ([Databento](https://databento.com/pricing)).

## Slow, diversified sleeves beat day-trading setups

Nick's results fit the literature's base rate. In the anomaly literature, **65% of 452 published anomalies fail at |t| ≥ 1.96** once microcaps are value-weighted away, and 82% fail at a multiple-testing hurdle ([Alpha Architect on Hou, Xue, Zhang](https://alphaarchitect.com/replicating-anomalies/)). Anomaly returns that survive decay about **58% after publication** ([CXO on McLean & Pontiff](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/effects-of-market-adaptation)). The counterweight is that the major themes (momentum, value, low-risk, quality) still replicate across 93 countries when built carefully ([NBER, Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28432)). Short-hold, single-stock technical rules from practitioners have no replicated support, and they are exactly what failed in Nick's rounds 1–2. The overnight/intraday decomposition shows why opening-range breakouts are structurally disadvantaged. **Momentum and reversal profits are earned overnight**, while institutions trade against momentum during the day ([LSE, Lou-Polk-Skouras](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/87481)). An engine that enters near the close captures the leg that pays, and one that trades the open fights the tug of war.

The sleeves complement each other. Trend and momentum buy winners on monthly signals, while RSI(2) buys short-term losers inside an uptrend, so the mean-reversion sleeve earns in the pullbacks that hurt the momentum core. That diversification claim is an inference: no published correlation matrix across these sleeves was found, so Nick's backtests must compute it. The open research item is whether Nick's SPY RSI(2) edge transfers to individual S&P 500 names. No survivorship-free constituent-level test was found in the literature. The academic analogue, large-cap residual reversal, survives costs only with tight turnover control ([CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=6251); [de Groot et al.](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/25718)). The test needs point-in-time index membership, next-close or close-auction fills, and at least 5–10 bp round-trip costs. RSI(2)'s tail is real: the leveraged SSO version lost 40.4% on a single trade in late 2008 ([CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=26735)). Every mean-reversion position needs a time stop, a catastrophic stop and no leverage.

## What to copy, avoid and build

**Copy** the plumbing that ai-hedge-fund, Vibe-Trading and the evaluation literature converged on: hard clamps the signal layer cannot override, abstain-on-failure, fail-loud data contracts, an intent-before-write order gate, an idempotent kill switch, and a hash-chained ledger. Add next-close entry, settle-then-reflect review, and CLQT-style evaluation: point-in-time data, cost tiers, repeated runs and a reported noise floor ([CLQT](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.29771v1)). Copy the PASS/BLOCK risk check and file-based state from day-trader-skill, and the shadow-before-promotion gate from Polyphemus. **Avoid** persona investors, multi-round debates (The Alpha Illusion finds "debate is not equivalent to aggregating independent experts" ([alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.16895.md))), verbal LLM confidence used as a probability, any pre-cutoff LLM backtest, intraday breakout setups, and leverage on mean reversion. Also avoid trusting a scheduled run's "Succeeded" status without reading the transcript.

**Build** a four-layer system. Layer one is a deterministic signal engine in Python covering the three sleeves above, rebalanced monthly (trend, momentum) or daily at the close (RSI(2)). Every number is computed in code, and the engine is evaluated against SPY, an equal-weight universe and a plain momentum rule, net of costs. Layer two is a risk module with a per-name cap, gross cap and trade-count cap. It also enforces volatility-scaled sizing, catastrophic and time stops, a `buying_power` ceiling, and clamps that go to cash and are logged. Layer three is an execution service holding Nick's own keys, initially Alpaca, with tastytrade as the fallback. It records intent durably before each broker write, uses idempotent client order IDs, and reconciles positions after every run. It treats any malformed or failed broker response as a halt, never as an empty account. Layer four is Claude. It runs the full paper and shadow book end to end and backtests candidate sleeves under the validation standard (published edge halved, large-cap only, survivorship-free, net of costs, positive post-2010 out of sample). It also checks the ledger daily against the broker, writes the weekly review, and can contribute one bounded LLM feature. That feature is a news and earnings veto that may only *remove* a mean-reversion entry, run blind, and earn a role only from post-cutoff forward evidence.

Nick wants Robinhood specifically, which leaves a short two-way choice. Option one runs the real-money engine on Alpaca and keeps Robinhood for read-only monitoring through the MCP; this is fully autonomous today on documented, sandboxed APIs. Option two keeps Robinhood with trade approvals ON. The engine (or Claude's shadow run) produces the day's order list, and Nick approves each order in the app. This keeps Robinhood but gives up full autonomy and lives within cash-account settlement. Either way, promotion to real money should follow a fixed gate: at least 50 completed shadow trades, as Polyphemus requires, zero unreconciled ledger breaks, and live-versus-backtest Sharpe within CLQT's roughly 0.2 contamination threshold. Initial size should be small enough that a year of noise costs little, since the ±0.42 Sharpe noise floor means a convincing record takes years.

## Conclusion

The research turns a question about which AI trader to copy into an engineering answer. The edge comes from boring, replicated, low-turnover factor themes executed near the close. The safety comes from code the LLM cannot override, and the LLM's real comparative advantage in Nick's system is supervision rather than decision-making. Claude's refusal to place real-money orders turns out to match what the evidence recommends anyway. The arenas show LLMs failing on timing, sizing and overtrading, and the benchmarks show scaffolding mattering more than the model. Putting Claude on research, paper trading, reconciliation and review uses it where builders report it works, and keeps it away from where the evidence says it loses money.

Two unknowns should drive the next research round. The first is whether RSI(2) survives at the constituent level on a survivorship-free S&P 500 universe; if it does, it becomes the multi-stock satellite Nick is looking for. The second is whether Robinhood documents its MCP auth and adds a paper mode, which would turn option two into a real option one. Until both resolve, the defensible move is to start shadow trading the monthly sleeves now: every month of forward record is worth more than any framework's backtest.
